Who was Dr. Clarence Gonstead?

A Wisconsin chiropractor and trained automotive engineer who graduated Palmer College in 1923 and built an analysis around the intervertebral disc.

The method is named after Clarence Gonstead, D.C., who came to chiropractic first as a patient and then as an engineer, and who spent a career arguing that the profession had misread where the problem starts.

Born in 1898 in Willow Lake, South Dakota, he grew up on a family dairy farm in Primrose, Wisconsin. At nineteen he was severely ill with rheumatoid arthritis, and chiropractic care was what got him back on his feet. He enrolled at Palmer College of Chiropractic and graduated in 1923.

He also trained and worked as an automotive engineer, and that is the part that matters to the method. He looked at the spine the way an engineer looks at a structure: as a system under load, where a fault at one point changes how everything above and below it behaves.

The disagreement that produced the method

The prevailing view in the profession located the problem in the position of the vertebra. Gonstead argued the intervertebral disc was where it began, and built an analysis around finding disc-level involvement precisely rather than inferring it. The five components exist because that argument required evidence.

He is widely quoted as telling students: you may question your application, but never question the principle. It is repeated often enough in Gonstead teaching that it survives him as a summary of the method's discipline.
